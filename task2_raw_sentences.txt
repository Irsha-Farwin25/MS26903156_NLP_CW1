Excessive screen use among preschool children is raising concerns over their brain development, language and social skills, physical activity and overall health, with 64% of children spending more than 30 minutes a day on screens, according to a recent survey conducted by the Health Promotion Bureau in collaboration with the University of Colombo and UNICEF.
The figures were highlighted by Dr Anil Jasinghe, Secretary to the Ministry of Health and Mass Media, at a media briefing.
The survey, conducted across four provinces - Southern, Western, Central and Northern - involved around 1,100 children, their parents and preschool teachers from 55 preschools.
According to the survey, 64% of children spend more than 30 minutes a day on screens, while approximately 43% consume their meals while using a screen.
Around 32% of the children also sleep for less than the recommended 10 to 11 hours a day.
According to World Health Organisation recommendations, children below two years should not be exposed to screens, including televisions, mobile phones, tablets and computers.
For children between two and five years, screen time should not exceed 60 minutes a day and should preferably be limited to 30 to 45 minutes per day, according to Dr Enoka Wickramasinghe, Consultant Community Physician and Head of the National Preschool Health Promotion Programme at the Health Promotion Bureau of the Ministry of Health and Mass Media.
Dr Wickramasinghe said excessive passive screen use could affect early childhood development, particularly as the first few years of life are a period of rapid brain growth and development.
She explained that when children passively watch screens, they do not receive the same stimulation through their five senses as they would through active play and interaction.
“When a child is not stimulated through the five senses, neuronal connections will not be developed in the brain,” Dr Wickramasinghe said.
She said this could affect areas of development, including cognitive, language, motor, emotional and social development, as children require active experiences and interaction to develop these skills.
Dr Wickramasinghe also pointed to concerns raised by psychologists and psychiatrists over language development and behavioural problems among children who spend excessive amounts of time in front of screens.
“Language abilities can be poor, and language development can be reduced remarkably. There can also be behaviour problems, such as aggressive behaviour,” she said.
She stressed, however, that screen use does not cause autism, while noting that children who spend excessive periods in front of screens may have fewer opportunities to develop social connections and social skills.
Another concern is the impact of prolonged screen use on children's physical activity.
Dr Wickramasinghe said spending extended periods seated in front of screens contributes to sedentary behaviour, which may have longer-term consequences for children's health.
According to her, children should engage in at least 180 minutes of physical activity a day, including around 60 minutes of vigorous activity.
She noted that remaining seated for more than one hour is considered sedentary or inactive.
Further, reduced physical activity can increase the risk of overweight and obesity and contribute to non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, high blood pressure and heart diseases later in life, she said.
She also identified eye-related problems as another possible concern associated with excessive screen exposure among young children.
Meanwhile, the survey also found that approximately 43% of children consume their meals while using screens, highlighting concerns over eating habits, as children become distracted from eating when using a screen during meals.
The survey has now formed the baseline for a new social and behaviour change communication programme under the Health Promotion Bureau’s National Preschool Health Promotion Programme.
Dr Wickramasinghe said the programme aims to improve eight key health behaviours among preschool children and their caregivers, including parents, teachers and other family members, with reduced screen time identified as one of the eight behaviours requiring attention.
She further added that parents and caregivers are encouraged to provide children with toys, storybooks and other age-appropriate activities to keep them engaged and entertained without relying on screens, while spending more time playing and interacting with children themselves.

The government has dismissed reports alleging that it is taking steps to curtail rooftop solar power while promoting other energy sources.
Energy Minister Anura Karunathilake, speaking at a news briefing today, said the allegations were baseless and pointed to the government's efforts to expand solar power generation across the country.
According to the Minister, the government has facilitated the addition of 1,513 MW of rooftop and ground-mounted solar capacity to the national grid over the past two years.
He said rooftop solar accounted for 1,141.1 MW of the capacity added to the grid during 2025 and 2026, while a further 534 MW came from ground-mounted solar projects.
The government plans to increase solar power capacity connected to the national grid by a further 4,585.7 MW by 2029, the Minister said.
For 2027, plans are in place to add 1,392 MW of solar power to the national grid, including 500 MW from rooftop solar and 642 MW from ground-mounted solar projects.
Drug trafficking networks are using different maritime routes to smuggle various types of narcotics and other contraband into Sri Lanka, with the Northern Sea and Arabian Sea emerging as key routes for different categories of illicit cargo, according to the Sri Lanka Navy.
The Sri Lanka Navy has seized illicit drugs estimated to be worth more than Rs. 55 billion through maritime and coastal anti-narcotics operations conducted so far this year, Navy Spokesman Commander Chaminda Walakuluge said.
According to the Navy, around 300 suspects and 50 vessels allegedly linked to local and international drug trafficking networks have been taken into custody during the operations.
Detailing the massive hauls recovered during these operations, the Navy Spokesman said that forces had intercepted over 1,080 kilograms of heroin, 590 kilograms of crystal methamphetamine (commonly known as Ice), and 256 kilograms of cocaine.
In addition to these highly addictive substances, the Navy confiscated over four million narcotic pills, 51 kilograms of hashish, and a staggering 55,000 kilograms (55 tonnes) of smuggled beedi leaves.
A recent operation in the Gurunagar lagoon area resulted in the detection of around 800 kilograms of cannabis.
Drug trafficking networks use different maritime routes to bring contraband into Sri Lanka.
Cannabis and beedi leaves are mainly smuggled through the Northern Sea, while heroin, crystal methamphetamine and cocaine are primarily transported through the Arabian Sea.
The Navy has strengthened surveillance and interception operations in these areas to prevent illegal consignments from reaching the shore, he said.
Alongside maritime enforcement, the Navy is also conducting community awareness programmes aimed at preventing drug abuse, particularly among young people.
The Navy said its operations are focused on disrupting drug trafficking networks, supporting legal action against those involved in the trade and protecting communities from the impact of illicit drugs.

A new Pre-Medical Foundation Certificate Course will shortly be launched by the Faculty of Medical Sciences, Lyceum Campus, No. 10 Raymond Road, Nugegoda, in their state-of-the-art medical teaching facility.
The course is designed to help A-Level science students make a smoother and more confident transition from school-based science learning to medical education.
Prof. Mohan de Silva, a Senior Consultant Surgeon and the President, Lyceum Campus explains the purpose of introducing a such a program by the Lyceum Campus.
For aspiring students, entering medical school is both an achievement and a challenge.
This is true not only for students who gain admission to state medical faculties, but also for those planning to study medicine overseas.
During the past decade, medical education and training have changed rapidly in response to the growth of scientific knowledge, new educational technologies, advanced diagnostic and treatment methods, minimally invasive and virtual procedures, and the increasing use of artificial intelligence.
After years of preparing for examinations through a conventional biology-based curriculum, A-Level science students are suddenly expected to learn medicine through an integrated approach.
In medical school, the basic sciences taught during the pre-clinical years - Anatomy, Physiology and Biochemistry - lead into para-clinical subjects such as Pathology and Pharmacology, and later into clinical training, where students learn to apply knowledge to disease conditions, by learning the art of clinical reasoning with effective communication, professionalism and empathic approach.
These are not separate compartments; they are connected parts of understanding the human body and disease, eventually leading to understanding the pathway towards total patient care.
The central idea of the programme is simple: “How the sciences you already know become medicine.”
Students are guided from their A-Level knowledge towards understanding the most relevant aspects of normal human structure, normal function, biochemical processes, disturbed structure and function, clinical manifestations, an introduction to disease conditions, and finally the basic principles of clinical decision-making.
The course runs for three months, three days a week, with six hours of learning each day.
It includes 290 guided learning hours, of which 288 are scheduled contact hours and two hours are set aside for induction and portfolio orientation.
The programme will be delivered by a consultant-led teaching team, including consultant surgeons, consultant anaesthetists, academic and clinical professors, pathologists, medical biochemists, research experts, IT and digital-learning experts, and English communication specialists.
The programme is arranged into seven carefully planned modules.
Students begin with an introduction to human anatomy and physiology, then move into regional anatomy integrated with physiology, biochemistry and the fundamentals of pathology.
They next learn how normal structure and function change in disease, followed by clinical orientation that introduces the art of applying basic sciences to clinical conditions.
The course also includes resuscitation and principles of critical care, basic research methodology, the use of IT and AI in learning medicine, and personal development for future medical students.
A special feature of the course is its emphasis on integrated and clinically relevant learning.
Instead of memorising Anatomy, Physiology and Biochemistry as isolated subjects, students learn how these sciences help explain real clinical problems.
For example, they may study the course of a nerve and then understand how injury to that nerve produces weakness, sensory changes and clinical signs.
They may learn how the lungs exchange gases and then apply that knowledge to asthma or shortness of breath.
They may study blood glucose regulation and then understand the basis of diabetes and laboratory investigations such as HbA1c.
Students are also introduced to the way doctors think.
Through simple clinical scenarios, small-group discussions, models, prosected specimens, imaging, basic clinical observations and skills-lab activities, they learn to ask important questions: What is the problem?
What evidence is needed to support the diagnosis?
Alongside the sciences, the course gives attention to Medical English, scientific communication, writing skills, research literacy, digital learning, teamwork, feedback, time management, examination preparation and professional behaviour.
Students maintain a personal medical vocabulary portfolio and practise explaining scientific and clinical ideas in clear language.
This is especially important because success in medicine requires not only knowledge, but also communication, curiosity, discipline and the ability to learn independently.
The programme is designed to reflect the integrated nature of medical learning.
Assessment includes anatomy practical work, simple integrated clinical cases, clinical skills, research and critical appraisal, Medical English, professionalism, teamwork and a final integrated assessment.
This helps students appreciate medicine as a connected discipline rather than a collection of unrelated facts.
For parents and students considering a future in medicine, this course offers more than academic preparation.
It offers early exposure to medical language, the thinking style of doctors and the expectations of medical school in line with global trends.
It helps students understand why basic sciences matter, how diseases develop, and how doctors use evidence to make decisions.
Most importantly, it aims to replace fear and uncertainty with confidence, familiarity and readiness to learn.
Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says his country has proposed a deal to the US that would see the Strait of Hormuz reopened within a week.
Araghchi told reporters at the UN in New York that this could be done "if the necessary conditions are met" and said these were contained within the memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed in June.
"The choice now rests with the United States," he said, adding the plan was delivered to the US via Qatar.
Asked about the Iranian offer, a US official told the BBC positive discussions were taking place through mediators, including about nuclear issues.
Earlier this week, US President Donald Trump threatened to "annihilate the Islamic Republic".
The US and Israel launched air strikes on Iran on 28 February.
Iran responded by attacking Israel and US bases in the Gulf and Arab Gulf allies of the US - as well as blocking the Strait of Hormuz.
Some 20% of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas used to pass through the strait before the war and its closure has caused wild fluctuations of energy prices.
It has also increased pressure on Trump to find a way of ending the conflict - particularly as the US heads to Congressional elections in November.
"If the necessary conditions are met, the strait can be reopened, a normal maritime passage restored within seven days," Araghchi told reporters.
He did not specify what steps Iran wanted the US to take but added that "the actions that the United States should take are not new. They are all already in the MOU".
"I can tell you if there is seriousness in the US side to come to a deal and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, everything is now prepared," he added.
A US official told the BBC that the US was "in a very strong position with control of the Strait of Hormuz, so we are not in a rush".
In an interview with CBS News, the BBC's US partner, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said that the ceasefire process "will begin from the very day they accept it".
"We had previously reached an agreement based on the MOU that was signed in Pakistan... if the United States agrees to proceed in that way, everything will return to the way it was before," he added.
Pezeshkian also said Iran would now allow UN nuclear inspectors into the country.
Rejecting claims that Iran is trying to develop a nuclear weapon, the Iranian president said: "If they wish to come in and have inspections, we can reach accords and agreements vis-à-vis that specific topic within the negotiations."
The MOU between the US and Iran, signed on 18 June, was designed to bring about a ceasefire and at least partly reopen the Strait of Hormuz.
The 14-point agreement said Iran would never have a nuclear weapon.
It noted that the US, Iran and their allies would end military operations on "all fronts" and contained provisions including the end of a US naval blockade and sanctions relief.
It also said Iran would "make arrangements using its best efforts for the safe passage of commercial vessels" through the strait and hold talks with Oman over future shipping arrangements.
But it fell apart a short time later when both sides resumed strikes.
Iran has attacked vessels it says have attempted to cross the Strait of Hormuz without authorisation.
The US has attacked Iranian sites in the strait - as well as Iranian tankers off the country's coast.
It has also maintained a blockade of Iranian ports, causing huge disruption to the Iranian economy.
Trump since has claimed the US has been successful in getting oil out through the Strait of Hormuz.

The National Building Research Organisation (NBRO) has issued the highest-level landslide evacuation warning for several areas in Galle, Kandy and Nuwara Eliya districts amid continued heavy rainfall and an increased risk of landslides.
The Level 3 (Red) warning is effective from 4.00 p.m. today (25) until 4.00 p.m. tomorrow (26).
In Galle, the warning covers the Neluwa, Nagoda, Niyagama and Thawalama Divisional Secretariat divisions and surrounding areas.
In Kandy, Ganga Ihala Korale, Udapalatha, Doluwa and Pasbage Korale have been placed under the Red warning.
In Nuwara Eliya, the warning applies to Kothmale West, Ambagamuwa, Kotmale East and Norwood.
The NBRO has advised residents in areas under the Level 3 warning to evacuate to safe locations if rainfall continues, citing the possibility of landslides, slope failures, rockfalls, failures of cut slopes and ground subsidence.
Meanwhile, Level 2 (Amber) warnings have been issued for Elpitiya, Baddegama and Karandeniya in Galle; Walallawita in Kalutara; Deltota, Udunuwara, Gangawata Korale, Yatinuwara and Panvila in Kandy; and Aranayake, Dehiowita, Mawanella, Deraniyagala and Yatiyanthota in Kegalle.
Thalawakele in Nuwara Eliya, as well as Ratnapura and Pelmadulla in Ratnapura District, are also under the Amber warning.
Pelmadulla has been upgraded from the previous warning level.
Level 1 (Yellow) warnings remain in force for several other areas in the Colombo, Gampaha, Hambantota, Kalutara, Kandy, Kegalle, Kurunegala, Matara, Nuwara Eliya and Ratnapura districts.
The Nuwara Eliya Divisional Secretariat division has been newly added to the Yellow warning list.
The NBRO said the warning levels were determined based on cumulative rainfall recorded in the respective areas and weather forecasts issued by the Department of Meteorology.
It urged the public to exercise particular caution in areas with steep slopes, valleys, man-made cut slopes and roads in potentially hazardous locations, especially areas previously identified as being vulnerable to landslides.
Residents in landslide-prone areas have also been advised to remain alert for warning signs, including cracks or subsidence in the ground, leaning trees, electricity or telephone poles, cracks appearing in buildings on slopes, and the sudden emergence of springs or muddy water.
